What the data shows. Seven setups on seven tasks: the four pilot tasks (two runs per cell on three of them, one on the smoke task) and three harder ones, each run once (19 new runs; two cells, the team and the swarm on the data task, were not run because the $10 step cap was within 10%). On the three new tasks the table separates the contestants more than the pilot did, but not by finishing. Only 6 of the 19 new runs finished inside the caps (Opus and Sonnet on the debugging task, Sonnet's failing the check; Sol, Sonnet, Opus and Astra on the data task); the other 13 were killed at the 400,000-token cap, every one at the token cap and none at the dollar or minute caps. For those, the report's "work passes the check" column is the fairer view: it says whether the hidden check passed on the files the run had written when it was killed.

Separation 1: finding the root cause. On the debugging task (a mypy crash from a user's traceback) five setups edited the semantic-analysis code where the bug starts, with 8 to 23 changed lines against the maintainers' 12: Opus, Sol, Astra and both Opus teams. Haiku and Sonnet instead patched the code the traceback ended in (typeops.py; Haiku also subtypes.py). That removes the crash but gives the wrong type, and the maintainers' regression cases failed for both. Only Opus also finished inside the caps (174 s, $0.41); Sol, Astra and the two teams were killed at 413,000 to 438,000 tokens with work that passes. So on this task the real split is Haiku and Sonnet against everyone else, and it is a split in where the fix goes, not in speed.

Separation 2: bulk and detail, and no clean winner. On the multi-file feature (an HTTP conditional-requests rework, 864 changed source lines upstream) all seven runs hit the token cap, between 401,000 and 453,000 tokens, after 98 to 413 seconds. The hidden check passes on the work left by Sol, Sonnet, Opus, Astra and the Opus + Sonnet team (5 of 7); it fails on Haiku (one file, 149 lines) and on the Opus + Haiku swarm. A pass rate of 0 of 7 here measures the cap, not the models. Fresh tokens (input, cache writes and output, without cache reads) were only 57,000 to 98,000 in every one of these runs; the 400,000 is almost all cache reads, so a cap on fresh tokens or on dollars would have let several of them finish. Dollar cost of these runs ran from $0.03 (Haiku) to $1.32 (Astra), against dollar caps that never bit.

What still does not separate: the data task. Four setups finished and passed it (Sol 91 s, Sonnet 45 s, Opus 134 s, Astra 84 s) and Haiku's work passed too before the cap cut it at 97 s. Two other data tasks built for this step (an IRS charity-list task with a precise spec, and a SEC annual-revenue extraction with several latent traps) were solved by both Haiku and Sonnet in 15 to 55 seconds and were dropped as too easy, and the pilot's data task was passed by everyone. Four messy-data tasks, one verdict: these models handle merge, date and unit traps in about a minute, and the last one is kept only as the least easy of the three that were tried.

Solo against team against swarm (n = 1, a hint only). On the debugging task Opus alone was cheaper than the Opus + Sonnet team ($0.41 against $0.71) and finished, where the team and the swarm ($0.62, 13 workers) were killed with passing work. On the feature task the team cost about what Opus did ($0.85 against $0.94, both killed with passing work) and the swarm cost $0.35 but its work failed the check. Nothing here shows that parallel workers help on a task of this size.

Selection effect. The three tasks were kept or replaced after probing them with Haiku and Sonnet (one run each, listed in evidence/m3-tasks.md), so those two were disadvantaged by construction and the other five were not. Their failures on the debugging task should be read with that in mind; the fact that five different setups solved the same task shows the task is solvable.

What the data does not show. It does not rank the models (the token cap decides who counts as passing on two of three tasks), it shows nothing about reliability (one run per cell), and it does not support the study's estimate that these tasks take a strong agent 10 to 30 minutes: the slowest run was 7 minutes and the strongest solutions took 2 to 3 minutes. Next step, before any wider run: measure the cap on fresh tokens or dollars instead of total tokens, rerun the feature task and the two empty cells, and look for tasks where the strongest contestant, not only the cheapest, needs more than a few minutes.
